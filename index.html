How to Read a Supplement's Certificate of Analysis

A certificate of analysis — a COA — is the most useful document in the supplement world, and the least read. It is a laboratory report for a specific batch of a product, listing what was tested and what the results were. Learning to read one takes a few minutes, and it turns a brand's quality claims from promises into checkable facts.

Start with the identity of the document. A real COA names the product, the batch or lot number, the date of testing, and the laboratory that performed the tests. If any of these is missing — no batch number, no date, no laboratory named — the document cannot be tied to the bottle in your hand, and its value drops sharply. A certificate for a different batch, or from an unnamed laboratory, is not evidence for your product.

Next, check what was tested. A thorough COA covers three things: identity, potency and purity. Identity testing confirms the ingredient is what the label says it is — the right plant, the right compound, the right form. Potency testing measures the amount of active ingredient and compares it with the label claim. Purity testing looks for contaminants: heavy metals, microbes, and sometimes pesticides or solvents. A COA that covers only one of these is incomplete, and a brand that publishes only the favourable section is not being fully transparent.

Then look at the results themselves, not just the pass/fail column. A potency result of 98 per cent of the label claim is a good result; a result of 75 per cent is not, even if the laboratory marked it as a pass against a loose specification. Contaminant results should be reported as actual numbers, not just "pass" — and they should be compared with the relevant limits, which the COA should cite. Numbers you can read are evidence; a tick in a box is a summary.

Also check the standard. The COA should state which standard the product was tested against — a pharmacopeial monograph, an industry standard, or the manufacturer's own specification. A product tested against a recognised standard carries more weight than one tested against a specification the manufacturer wrote itself, since the manufacturer chooses its own pass mark.

Finally, ask for the COA if it is not published. Brands that test every batch should be able to provide the certificate for your batch on request — and many will publish them online, searchable by batch number. A brand that refuses, delays, or replies with a generic quality statement has answered your question in its own way.

A COA is a laboratory's word, not a brand's. Ask for it, read it, and let the numbers — not the promise — decide.
